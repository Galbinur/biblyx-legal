French looks easier on the page than it sounds: you recognise *gouvernement*, *problème* and *possible* long before you can catch them in speech. That makes reading the natural first door into French. It also teaches what listening hides, such as the silent letters, the genders and the past tense of written stories, the *passé simple*, which you will meet in almost every novel and hardly ever hear. This guide gives you French books for each level from A1 to B2, where to get them legally for nothing, and how to read them on a phone without stopping at every unknown word.

## How to tell whether a French book is right for you

Open the first page and count the words you don't know. Zero or one: too easy. Two to five: right, you follow the story and still learn. Six or more: keep it for later. Don't count verb forms you recognise but can't name yet, like *il fut* or *elle vint*; that is the *passé simple*, and it becomes familiar quickly.

## A1–A2: graded readers, fables and tales

- **Graded readers:** series such as *Lectures CLE en français facile* (CLE International) and *Lire en français facile* (Hachette) retell stories with a limited vocabulary, by level, often with audio. They cost money but are written exactly for this stage.
- **La Fontaine's fables and Perrault's fairy tales** (*Le Petit Chaperon rouge*, *Cendrillon*): you know the stories, so the words are easy to guess.
- **Easy news:** RFI's *Le journal en français facile* and TV5Monde's learning pages give short texts and audio for learners.

## B1: real short stories

- **Alphonse Daudet, *La Chèvre de M. Seguin*.** A goat who wants freedom; short and moving. It comes with Biblyx.
- **Guy de Maupassant, *La Parure*.** A famous story with a twist ending. It comes with Biblyx.
- **Daudet's *Lettres de mon moulin*.** The collection *La Chèvre de M. Seguin* comes from; each story stands alone.
- **Maupassant's other short stories**, once one feels easy: there are about three hundred.

## B2: novels and novellas

- **Jules Verne, *Le Tour du monde en quatre-vingts jours*.** An adventure with short chapters and a clear plot.
- **Guy de Maupassant, *Le Horla*.** A diary of growing fear; the language is rich but the form is simple. It comes with Biblyx.
- **Alexandre Dumas, *Les Trois Mousquetaires*.** Long, but full of dialogue and action.
- **Gustave Flaubert, *Un cœur simple*.** A novella in precise, beautiful French; a step towards the great classics.

Classics are free because they are old, and they use the *passé simple* everywhere. At B2 that is no problem; at A2 it is tiring, so stay with graded readers and tales until then.

## Where to get French books free and legally

- **Project Gutenberg** (gutenberg.org): out-of-copyright books as EPUB and plain text, with a French section that includes Verne, Maupassant, Daudet and Dumas.
- **Gallica** (gallica.bnf.fr): the French National Library's free digital library.
- **Bibliothèque numérique romande** and **Wikisource** (fr.wikisource.org): carefully made public-domain EPUBs and texts.
- **LibriVox**: free audiobooks read by volunteers, including many in French. Listen while you read.
- **A warning about apps and libraries:** books bought in Kindle or Apple Books, and books borrowed through a library app, are locked to those apps and won't open in another reader. EPUBs from Gutenberg and DRM-free shops open anywhere.

## How to read French on your phone

The classic problem: an unknown word, a dictionary tab, a lost sentence, and the book closes at the third word. With **Biblyx** on Android you open your own EPUB, PDF or TXT, or paste a French article, and tap a word. You see what it means in that sentence, with its part of speech, base form and an estimated CEFR level: tap *fut* and you see it is *être*. Nouns come with their article (*le*, *la*), so you learn the gender with the word, and when a word belongs to an expression such as *avoir beau*, you see both meanings. One more tap saves the word with its sentence as a flashcard, which comes back after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. Reading and review work offline; looking up a new word needs a connection. That is the whole idea: **Learn languages by reading your own books**.

Whatever app you use, the habit is the same:

1. Read 10 to 20 minutes a day.
2. Look up only the words that block the meaning; guess the rest from context and from English.
3. Save 5 to 10 words a day, not 50, and review them before your next session.
4. Save every noun with its article; French gender is learned word by word.
5. Read aloud now and then. It is the bridge between the French you read and the French you hear.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you learn French by reading books?

Reading builds vocabulary, grammar and spelling faster than almost anything else. French pronunciation needs listening, so pair your book with its LibriVox recording or an audiobook.

### What is the passé simple, and do I need to learn it?

It is the past tense of written stories: *il fut*, *elle alla*, *ils prirent*. You don't need to use it, only to recognise it, and reading teaches that in a few weeks.

### What should I read first in French?

A graded reader at your level or Perrault's tales, then Daudet's *La Chèvre de M. Seguin* and Maupassant's *La Parure*. Save the long novels until short stories feel easy.

### Is Le Petit Prince good for learning French?

Many learners start with it because the language is simple, but it is not yet in the public domain everywhere, so buy or borrow a copy rather than download one.

### Where can I download French books for beginners for free?

Legally, from Project Gutenberg, Gallica, the Bibliothèque numérique romande and Wikisource. Sites that give away new bestsellers "for free" are pirate sites.
