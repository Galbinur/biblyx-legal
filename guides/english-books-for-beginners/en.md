Reading is the cheapest way to get a lot of English into your head, and the hardest part is not the reading. It is choosing a book you can actually finish. This guide gives you a short list for each level from A1 to B2, tells you where to get the books legally for free, and shows how to read them on a phone so that unknown words don't stop you.

## First, find your level in two minutes

You don't need a test. Open the first page of a book and count the words you don't know:

- **0–1 unknown words on a page:** too easy. Pick something harder.
- **2–5 unknown words:** right. You understand the story and still learn.
- **6 or more:** too hard for now. Keep the book for later.

If you want a label: A1–A2 readers manage short, simple sentences and everyday words; B1 readers follow a plain story with some help; B2 readers can handle an adult novel with a dictionary nearby.

## A1–A2: short stories and tales

Start with stories you may already know in your own language. Knowing the plot lets you guess words.

- **Aesop's Fables.** Each fable is a few sentences. Hundreds of them, all free.
- **The Selfish Giant** by Oscar Wilde. A short fairy tale with simple, repeated sentences. It is one of the stories built into Biblyx.
- **The Happy Prince** by Oscar Wilde. A little longer, same gentle style.
- **Grimm's and Andersen's fairy tales** in English translation. You know *Hansel and Gretel* and *The Ugly Duckling*, so the words come easily.
- **Graded readers.** Series such as Oxford Bookworms (Starter to Stage 2) and Penguin Readers (Levels 1–2) retell famous books with a limited vocabulary. They cost money, but they are written exactly for this stage.

**Free on this site:** six well-known fairy tales retold in easy English for A1, plus classic stories up to B2, complete and free, with a short meaning behind every underlined word. Start with [English short stories for beginners](/english-short-stories/).

## B1: real stories with short chapters

- **The Gift of the Magi** by O. Henry. A famous short story with a twist; about 2,000 words. Built into Biblyx.
- **The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes** by Arthur Conan Doyle. Twelve separate stories, so you can stop after one. Old-fashioned words appear, but the plots are clear.
- **The Tell-Tale Heart** by Edgar Allan Poe. Short and intense; the vocabulary is harder than the length suggests, so it sits between B1 and B2. Built into Biblyx.
- **Animal Farm** by George Orwell. Plain language, short chapters. Public domain in the EU and the UK, still under copyright in the US.
- Modern paperbacks that B1 readers finish: *Charlotte's Web*, *Holes*, *Wonder*, *The Alchemist*, and the first Harry Potter book. These are not free, but they are everywhere in bookstores and libraries.

## B2: full novels

- **The Great Gatsby** by F. Scott Fitzgerald. About 47,000 words; public domain since 2021.
- **The Picture of Dorian Gray** by Oscar Wilde. Witty dialogue, longer sentences.
- **Dracula** by Bram Stoker. Written as letters and diary entries, which keeps each piece short.
- **Frankenstein** by Mary Shelley. Beautiful but older English; try a chapter first.
- Modern picks: *The Curious Incident of the Dog in the Night-Time*, *The Hunger Games*, *Never Let Me Go*.

A note on classics: they are free because they are old, and old English has words nobody uses today. That is fine at B2, and tiring at A2. Below B1, prefer tales and graded readers.

## Where to get English books free and legally

- **Project Gutenberg** (gutenberg.org): more than 70,000 books whose copyright has expired, as EPUB and plain text. Every classic listed above is there, except Animal Farm, which is still under copyright in the US.
- **Standard Ebooks** (standardebooks.org): public-domain books like these, carefully formatted, with good covers.
- **Wikisource** and the **Internet Archive**: more texts, including older translations of fairy tales.
- **LibriVox**: free audiobooks of public-domain books, read by volunteers. Listen while you read the text.
- **Your public library**: many lend e-books through apps such as Libby. One honest warning: library e-books and books bought from Kindle or Apple Books are locked to those apps. You can't open them in another reader. EPUBs from Gutenberg, Standard Ebooks and DRM-free shops open anywhere.
- **Not a book? Use articles.** Simple English Wikipedia, BBC Learning English and news sites written in levels give you short texts for a ten-minute session.

The classic problem: you meet an unknown word, open a dictionary, lose the sentence, and after three words you close the book. A reader built for learners solves this.

Reading and reviewing work offline; looking up new words needs a connection. That is the idea behind Biblyx: **Learn languages by reading your own books**.

Whatever app you use, the habit is the same:

1. Read 10–20 minutes a day, not two hours on Sunday.
2. Look up only the words that block the meaning. Guess the rest from context.
3. Save 5–10 words a day, not 50. Review them before the next session.
4. Finish books. A finished easy book teaches more than an abandoned hard one.
5. Reread. The second pass of a chapter is where the words settle.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I learn English just by reading?

Reading gives you vocabulary, grammar patterns and a feel for the language faster than most other activities, but speaking and listening need their own practice. Pair the book with its audiobook, and say new sentences out loud.

### What level is Harry Potter?

The first book is usually placed at B1–B2: everyday dialogue, but many invented words and British expressions. Most readers manage it after a few graded readers.

### Are PDFs good for learning?

Only if the PDF contains real text. Scanned PDFs are pictures of pages: you can't tap or copy a word. Prefer EPUB or TXT when you have the choice.

### Where can I download English books for beginners in PDF for free?

Legally, from Project Gutenberg, Standard Ebooks, Wikisource and the Internet Archive; all offer EPUB, and most offer PDF or plain text. Sites that give away current bestsellers "free" are pirate sites.

### How many new words should I save per day?

Five to ten. More than that and the review pile grows faster than you can clear it; the words you meet again and again in the book will stick anyway.
