German has a reputation for long words and three genders, and both look worse on a word list than in a story. In a sentence, *der*, *die* and *das* come with the noun, and a long compound like *Handschuhfach* falls apart into words you already know. That is why reading is one of the fastest ways to learn German vocabulary: you meet each word in a sentence that shows how it is used. This guide gives you German texts for each level from A1 to B2, where to get them legally for nothing, and how to read them on a phone without stopping at every unknown word.

## How to tell whether a German text is right for you

Open the first page and count the words you don't know. Zero or one: too easy, pick something harder. Two to five: right, you follow the story and still learn. Six or more: put it aside for later. German adds one more test: if you can't tell who does what in a sentence because the verb waits at the end, the text is above your level, however simple the words look.

## A1–A2: fairy tales and easy news

Start with stories you already know in your own language; knowing the plot lets you guess.

- **Grimm's fairy tales** (*Kinder- und Hausmärchen*): *Rotkäppchen*, *Hänsel und Gretel*, *Die Bremer Stadtmusikanten*. Short, repetitive, and in the original German. *Rotkäppchen* and *Hänsel und Gretel* come with Biblyx.
- **Graded readers for German learners** (DaF readers, *Leichte Lektüren* and similar series from publishers such as Klett and Hueber) retell stories with a limited vocabulary, by level. They cost money but are written exactly for this stage.
- **News in easy German:** Deutschlandfunk's *nachrichtenleicht.de* writes the week's news in short, simple sentences, and Deutsche Welle's *Learn German* site has texts and slowly spoken news by level. A short text every day beats a long one on Sunday.

## B1: real short stories

- **Franz Kafka, *Vor dem Gesetz*.** One page, simple words, a strange and famous story. It comes with Biblyx.
- **Franz Kafka, *Die Verwandlung*.** A man wakes up as an insect. The sentences get long, but the vocabulary is everyday.
- **Theodor Storm, *Immensee*.** A short, quiet love story in clear 19th-century prose.
- **The Grimms' longer tales and German legends**, once the short ones feel easy.

## B2: novellas and novels

- **Theodor Storm, *Der Schimmelreiter*.** A North Sea legend; regional words, but a gripping plot.
- **Theodor Fontane, *Effi Briest*.** A classic novel with long sentences; read it after a few shorter books.
- **Arthur Schnitzler, *Fräulein Else*.** Told entirely as a young woman's thoughts; modern-sounding and intense.
- **Stefan Zweig's novellas.** Clear, elegant prose.

A note on older German: classics are free because they are old, and some spellings are old too (*Thür* for *Tür*, *daß* for *dass*). It slows A2 readers down and hardly matters at B2.

## Where to get German books free and legally

- **Project Gutenberg** (gutenberg.org): out-of-copyright books as EPUB and plain text, with a German section that includes Kafka, Storm, Fontane and the Grimms.
- **Wikisource** (de.wikisource.org) and **Zeno.org**: large collections of public-domain German texts to read online or copy.
- **LibriVox**: free audiobooks of public-domain works read by volunteers, including many in German. Listen while you read.
- **Deutsche Welle** (learngerman.dw.com) and **nachrichtenleicht.de**: free texts and news written for learners.
- **A warning about apps and libraries:** books bought in Kindle or Apple Books, and books borrowed through a library app, are locked to those apps and won't open in another reader. EPUBs from Gutenberg and DRM-free shops open anywhere.

## How to read German on your phone

The classic problem: an unknown word, a dictionary tab, a lost sentence, and the book closes at the third word. With **Biblyx** on Android you open your own EPUB, PDF or TXT, or paste a German article, and tap a word. You see what it means in that sentence, with its part of speech, base form and an estimated CEFR level. German nouns come with their article, so you learn *das Fenster*, not just *Fenster*, and compound words are split into their parts. When a word belongs to an expression, you see both meanings. One more tap saves the word with its sentence as a flashcard, which comes back after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. Reading and review work offline; looking up a new word needs a connection. That is the whole idea: **Learn languages by reading your own books**.

Whatever app you use, the habit is the same:

1. Read 10 to 20 minutes a day.
2. Look up only the words that block the meaning; guess the rest from context.
3. Save 5 to 10 words a day, not 50, and review them before your next session.
4. Notice the article of every noun you save; it is half the work of German grammar.
5. Reread a chapter you found hard a week later. It will feel easier, and that is the vocabulary sticking.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you learn German by reading?

Reading builds vocabulary, a feel for word order and the genders of nouns faster than almost anything else. Speaking and listening need their own practice: pair a book with its LibriVox recording and read sentences aloud.

### What should I read first in German?

Grimm's fairy tales or a graded reader at your level, then Kafka's *Vor dem Gesetz*. A whole novel can wait until short stories feel easy.

### How do I remember der, die and das?

Never save a noun without its article, and review it in its sentence. In Biblyx every German noun comes with its article, so the card has it too.

### Are German books with the translation next to the text a good idea?

They help in the first weeks, but your eyes drift to your own language. Tapping single words keeps you reading German.

### Where can I find German books for beginners as PDF for free?

Legally, on Project Gutenberg, Wikisource, Zeno.org and Deutsche Welle's learning site. Sites that give away new bestsellers "for free" are pirate sites.
