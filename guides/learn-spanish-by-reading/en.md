Spanish is one of the friendliest languages to read early: it is written the way it sounds, and English speakers recognise thousands of words (*información*, *historia*, *posible*) on sight. The hard part is the verbs, and verbs are exactly what reading teaches best, because you see *fue*, *era* and *había sido* again and again in context. This guide gives you Spanish books for each level from A1 to B2, where to get them legally for nothing, and how to read them on a phone without stopping at every unknown word.

## How to tell whether a Spanish book is right for you

Open the first page and count the words you don't know. Zero or one: too easy. Two to five: right, you follow the story and still learn. Six or more: keep it for later. Ignore words you can guess from English; count only the ones that stop you.

## A1–A2: graded readers and short tales

- **Graded readers** (*lecturas graduadas*): publishers of Spanish-teaching books such as Edelsa and Edinumen sell short novels written for each level, often with audio. They cost money, but at A1–A2 nothing else is as well matched.
- **Horacio Quiroga, *Cuentos de la selva*.** Animal stories written for children, with simple sentences and a lot of repetition.
- **Fables and folk tales** in Spanish, such as Samaniego's and Iriarte's fables: a few lines each, easy to finish in one sitting.

## B1: real short stories

- **Clarín (Leopoldo Alas):** *¡Adiós, Cordera!*, two children, a cow and a railway line; short and moving. It comes with Biblyx.
- **Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer:** *El monte de las ánimas*, a ghost legend from his *Leyendas*; old-fashioned but clear. It comes with Biblyx.
- **Emilia Pardo Bazán:** *Adriana* and her other short stories, compact and clear. *Adriana* comes with Biblyx.
- **Bécquer's other *Leyendas*.** Once one is easy, the rest are a natural next step.

## B2: novels

- **Benito Pérez Galdós, *Marianela*.** A short novel with a clear plot; a classic first novel in Spanish.
- **Juan Valera, *Pepita Jiménez*.** Letters and dialogue that keep each section short.
- **Vicente Blasco Ibáñez, short stories and *La barraca*.** Vivid and direct, with some regional words from Valencia.
- **What to leave for later:** *Don Quijote* is a joy at C1, but its 17th-century Spanish tires a B2 reader.

Spain or Latin America? Read both. Vocabulary differs a little (*coche* and *carro*, *ordenador* and *computadora*), grammar hardly at all, and Biblyx shows the meaning for the sentence you are reading either way.

## Where to get Spanish books free and legally

- **Project Gutenberg** (gutenberg.org): out-of-copyright books as EPUB and plain text, with a Spanish section that includes Galdós, Bécquer, Clarín and Valera.
- **Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes** (cervantesvirtual.com): a large free library of Spanish and Latin American literature.
- **Wikisource** (es.wikisource.org): more public-domain texts to read online or copy.
- **LibriVox**: free audiobooks read by volunteers, including many in Spanish. Listen while you read.
- **A warning about apps and libraries:** books bought in Kindle or Apple Books, and books borrowed through a library app, are locked to those apps and won't open in another reader. EPUBs from Gutenberg and DRM-free shops open anywhere.

## How to read Spanish on your phone

The classic problem: an unknown word, a dictionary tab, a lost sentence, and the book closes at the third word. With **Biblyx** on Android you open your own EPUB, PDF or TXT, or paste a Spanish article, and tap a word. You see what it means in that sentence, with its part of speech, base form and an estimated CEFR level: tap *fue* and you learn whether it is a form of *ser* or *ir*, as the sentence decides. Nouns come with their article (*el*, *la*), and when a word belongs to an expression such as *echar de menos*, you see both meanings. One more tap saves the word with its sentence as a flashcard, which comes back after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days. Reading and review work offline; looking up a new word needs a connection. That is the whole idea: **Learn languages by reading your own books**.

Whatever app you use, the habit is the same:

1. Read 10 to 20 minutes a day.
2. Look up only the words that block the meaning; guess the rest from context and from English.
3. Save 5 to 10 words a day, not 50, and review them before your next session.
4. Save verb forms with their sentence; the sentence shows which tense it is.
5. Finish books. A finished easy book teaches more than an abandoned hard one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you learn Spanish by reading books?

Yes, for vocabulary, grammar patterns and a feel for the language; reading is where verb tenses become familiar. Speaking and listening need their own practice, so pair your book with an audiobook and read sentences aloud.

### What are the best books to learn Spanish for beginners?

Graded readers at your level first, then short stories such as Clarín's *¡Adiós, Cordera!* or Bécquer's legends, then a short novel like *Marianela*. The best book is the one you finish.

### Should I read Don Quijote?

Not yet. It is long and written in the Spanish of 1605. Come back to it at C1, or read an adapted edition.

### Is Latin American Spanish harder to read than Spain Spanish?

No. A few everyday words differ, but the grammar is the same, and context makes the rest clear.

### Where can I download Spanish books for beginners for free?

Legally, from Project Gutenberg, the Biblioteca Virtual Miguel de Cervantes and Wikisource. Sites that give away new bestsellers "for free" are pirate sites.
